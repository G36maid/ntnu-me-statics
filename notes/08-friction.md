## 8.1 Characteristics of Dry Friction

![ch08-Friction Slide 04](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-004.png)

**Friction** is a force that resists the movement of two contacting surfaces that slide relative to one another. It acts tangent to the surfaces at the points of contact and is directed so as to oppose the possible or existing motion.

**Dry friction** (Coulomb friction) occurs when there is no lubricating fluid between the surfaces.

![ch08-Friction Slide 05](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-005.png)

### Theory of Dry Friction

![ch08-Friction Slide 06](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-006.png)

Consider the effects caused by pulling horizontally on a block of uniform weight $W$ resting on a rough horizontal surface that is nonrigid or deformable.

![ch08-Friction Slide 07](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-007.png)

Normal forces $\Delta N_n$ and frictional forces $\Delta F_n$ act along the contacting surface. For equilibrium, normal forces act upward to balance the block's weight $W$; frictional forces act to the left to prevent force $P$ from moving the block to the right.

![ch08-Friction Slide 08](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-008.png)

Many microscopic irregularities exist between the two surfaces. Reactive forces $\Delta R_n$ are developed at each point of contact, each contributing both a frictional component $\Delta F_n$ and a normal component $\Delta N_n$.

### Equilibrium

![ch08-Friction Slide 09](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-009.png)

The effect of normal and frictional loadings is indicated by their resultants $N$ and $F$.

- $N$ acts at a distance $x$ to the right of the line of action of $W$.
- This location coincides with the centroid (geometric center) in order to balance the "tipping effect" caused by $P$.

![ch08-Friction Slide 10](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-010.png)

### Impending Motion

![ch08-Friction Slide 11](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-011.png)

As $P$ is slowly increased, $F$ correspondingly increases until it attains a certain maximum value $F_s$, called the **limiting static frictional force**.

When the block is in unstable equilibrium, further increase in $P$ will cause the block to move.

The limiting static frictional force $F_s$ is directly proportional to the resultant normal force $N$:

$$\boxed{F_s = \mu_s N}$$

where $\mu_s$ is the **coefficient of static friction**.

![ch08-Friction Slide 12](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-012.png)

When the block is on the verge of sliding, $N$ and $F_s$ combine to create the resultant $R_s$. The angle $\phi_s$ that $R_s$ makes with $N$ is called the **angle of static friction**:

$$\phi_s = \tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{F_s}{N}\right) = \tan^{-1}\!\mu_s$$

![ch08-Friction Slide 13](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-013.png)

![ch08-Friction Slide 14](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-014.png)

### Motion

![ch08-Friction Slide 15](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-015.png)

When $P$ is slightly greater than $F_s$, the frictional force drops to a smaller value $F_k$, called the **kinetic frictional force**.

The magnitude of the kinetic friction force is:

$$\boxed{F_k = \mu_k N}$$

where $\mu_k$ is the **coefficient of kinetic friction** (typically 25% smaller than $\mu_s$).

![ch08-Friction Slide 16](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-016.png)

$R_k$ is defined by $\phi_k$, the **angle of kinetic friction**:

$$\phi_k = \tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{F_k}{N}\right) = \tan^{-1}\!\mu_k$$

Note that $\phi_s \geq \phi_k$.

![ch08-Friction Slide 17](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-017.png)

### Summary of Friction Force States

![ch08-Friction Slide 18](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-018.png)

- $F$ is a **static frictional force** if equilibrium is maintained.
- $F$ is a **limiting static frictional force** $F_s$ when it reaches the maximum value needed to maintain equilibrium.
- $F$ is a **kinetic frictional force** $F_k$ when sliding occurs at the contacting surface.

## 8.2 Problems Involving Dry Friction

![ch08-Friction Slide 24](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-024.png)

Three types of mechanics problems involving dry friction:

1. **No apparent impending motion** (equilibrium problems)
2. **Impending motion at all points of contact**
3. **Impending motion at some points of contact**

### Type 1: No Apparent Impending Motion

![ch08-Friction Slide 25](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-025.png)

- Total number of unknowns = Total number of available equilibrium equations.
- Frictional forces must satisfy $F \leq \mu_s N$; otherwise, slipping will occur and the body will not remain in equilibrium.

![ch08-Friction Slide 26](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-026.png)

### Type 2: Impending Motion at All Points of Contact

![ch08-Friction Slide 27](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-027.png)

- Total number of unknowns = Total number of available equilibrium equations plus the total number of available frictional equations ($F = \mu N$).
- When motion is impending at the points of contact: $F_s = \mu_s N$; if the body is slipping: $F_k = \mu_k N$.

![ch08-Friction Slide 28](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-028.png)

**Example:** Find the smallest angle $\theta$ at which a 100-N bar can be placed against the wall without slipping. The five unknowns are determined from the three equilibrium equations and two static frictional equations at both points of contact: $F_A = 0.3 N_A$ and $F_B = 0.4 N_B$.

![ch08-Friction Slide 29](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-029.png)

### Type 3: Impending Motion at Some Points of Contact

![ch08-Friction Slide 30](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-030.png)

The number of unknowns will be less than the number of available equilibrium equations plus the number of available frictional equations. Several possibilities for motion or impending motion will exist, and the problem involves determining which kind of motion actually occurs.

![ch08-Friction Slide 31](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-031.png)

The actual situation can be determined by calculating $P$ for each case and choosing the case for which $P$ is smaller.

![ch08-Friction Slide 32](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-032.png)

### Equilibrium Versus Frictional Equations

![ch08-Friction Slide 33](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-033.png)

- The friction force $F$ is an "equilibrium force" and satisfies $F < \mu_s N$. You may assume the sense of direction of $F$ on the FBD.
- The correct sense is determined after solving the equilibrium equations. If $F$ is a negative scalar, the sense of $F$ is the reverse of that assumed.

![ch08-Friction Slide 34](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-034.png)

However, when the frictional equation $F = \mu N$ is used in the solution, the convenience of assuming the sense of $F$ is lost. $F$ must always act with its **correct sense** on the FBD whenever the frictional equation is used.

![ch08-Friction Slide 35](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-035.png)

## 8.3 Wedges

![ch08-Friction Slide 51](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-051.png)

A **wedge** is a simple machine used to transform an applied force into much larger forces, directed at approximately right angles to the applied force. Also used to slightly move or adjust heavy loads.

Consider the wedge used to lift a block by applying a force $P$ to the wedge.

![ch08-Friction Slide 52](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-052.png)

### Free-Body Diagrams

![ch08-Friction Slide 53](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-053.png)

- The weight of the wedge is neglected since it is small compared to the weight $W$ of the block.
- Frictional forces $F_1$ and $F_2$ must oppose the motion of the wedge.
- Frictional force $F_3$ of the wall on the block must act downward to oppose the block's upward motion.
- Locations of the resultant normal forces are not important since neither the block nor the wedge will tip.
- Moment equilibrium equations will not be considered.

### Analysis

![ch08-Friction Slide 54](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-054.png)

There are **7 unknowns**: force $P$ and 6 normal and frictional forces.

The **7 available equations** consist of:
- 4 force equilibrium equations ($\sum F_x = 0$, $\sum F_y = 0$ applied to both the wedge and block)
- 3 frictional equations ($F = \mu N$) applied at each surface of contact

If the block is to be **lowered**, the frictional forces act in the opposite sense.

The applied force $P$ will act to the right if the coefficient of friction is small or the wedge angle $\theta$ is large.

![ch08-Friction Slide 55](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-055.png)

Otherwise, $P$ may have a reverse sense of direction (pull on the wedge to remove it). If $P$ is not applied and friction forces hold the block in place, the wedge is referred to as **self-locking**.

## 8.4 Frictional Forces on Screws

![ch08-Friction Slide 59](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-059.png)

Screws are used as fasteners and sometimes to transmit power or motion from one part of a machine to another. A **square-threaded screw** can be thought of as an inclined plane (wedge) wrapped around a cylinder.

![ch08-Friction Slide 60](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-060.png)

### Thread Geometry

![ch08-Friction Slide 61](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-061.png)

Unwinding the thread by one revolution, the lead angle is:

$$\theta = \tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{l}{2\pi r}\right)$$

where $l$ is the lead (advance per revolution) and $r$ is the mean radius of the thread.

### Upward Impending Motion

![ch08-Friction Slide 62](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-062.png)

A square-threaded screw subjected to upward impending motion caused by the applied torsional moment $M$:

- $W$ is the vertical (axial) force on the thread.
- $M/r$ is the resultant horizontal force produced by the couple moment $M$.
- The reaction $R$ has both frictional and normal components, where $F = \mu_s N$.

![ch08-Friction Slide 63](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-063.png)

The angle of static friction is $\phi_s = \tan^{-1}(F/N) = \tan^{-1}\mu_s$.

Applying force equilibrium:

$$M = Wr\,\tan(\theta + \phi_s)$$

![ch08-Friction Slide 64](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-064.png)

### Self-Locking Screw

![ch08-Friction Slide 65](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-065.png)

A screw is **self-locking** if it remains in place under any axial load $W$ when the moment $M$ is removed. This requires:

$$\phi_s \geq \theta$$

If $\phi_s = \theta$, then $R$ acts vertically to balance $W$, and the screw is on the verge of winding downward.

### Downward Impending Motion ($\theta > \phi_s$)

![ch08-Friction Slide 66](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-066.png)

If the screw is not self-locking, a moment $M'$ is needed to **prevent** the screw from winding downward:

$$M' = Wr\,\tan(\theta - \phi_s)$$

### Downward Impending Motion ($\phi_s > \theta$)

![ch08-Friction Slide 67](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-067.png)

If the screw is self-locking, a couple moment $M''$ must be applied in the opposite direction to wind the screw downward:

$$M'' = Wr\,\tan(\phi_s - \theta)$$

## 8.5 Frictional Forces on Flat Belts

![ch08-Friction Slide 68](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-068.png)

Consider a flat belt passing over a fixed curved surface with total angle of contact equal to $\beta$ radians. The coefficient of friction is $\mu$ and $T_2 > T_1$.

![ch08-Friction Slide 69](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-069.png)

Normal and frictional forces vary both in magnitude and direction. The analysis requires studying forces on a **differential element** of the belt.

### Derivation

![ch08-Friction Slide 70](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-070.png)

Consider a FBD of a belt element having length $ds$. Assuming impending motion or motion of the belt, $dF = \mu\,dN$.

From equilibrium of the element:

$$\sum F_x = 0: \quad (T + dT)\cos\frac{d\theta}{2} - T\cos\frac{d\theta}{2} - dF = 0$$

$$\sum F_y = 0: \quad (T + dT)\sin\frac{d\theta}{2} + T\sin\frac{d\theta}{2} - dN = 0$$

![ch08-Friction Slide 71](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-071.png)

Simplifying (with $\cos\frac{d\theta}{2} \approx 1$, $\sin\frac{d\theta}{2} \approx \frac{d\theta}{2}$):

$$dT = dF = \mu\,dN$$

$$dN = T\,d\theta$$

Eliminating $dN$:

$$\frac{dT}{T} = \mu\,d\theta$$

Integrating over the contact angle $\beta$:

$$\int_{T_1}^{T_2}\frac{dT}{T} = \mu\int_0^\beta d\theta$$

$$\ln\frac{T_2}{T_1} = \mu\beta$$

$$\boxed{T_2 = T_1\,e^{\mu\beta}}$$

![ch08-Friction Slide 72](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-072.png)

## 8.6 Frictional Forces on Collar Bearings, Pivot Bearings and Disks

![ch08-Friction Slide 78](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-078.png)

Pivot and collar bearings are used to support axial load on a rotating shaft. Laws of dry friction are applied to determine the moment $M$ needed to turn the shaft when it supports an axial force $P$.

### Frictional Analysis

![ch08-Friction Slide 79](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-079.png)

The collar bearing is subjected to axial force $P$ with total contact area $\pi(R_2^2 - R_1^2)$.

The normal pressure $p$ is considered uniformly distributed:

$$p = \frac{P}{\pi(R_2^2 - R_1^2)}$$

![ch08-Friction Slide 80](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-080.png)

A differential area element $dA = r\,d\theta\,dr$ is subjected to a normal force $dN = p\,dA$ and an associated frictional force:

$$dF = \mu_s\,dN = \frac{\mu_s P}{\pi(R_2^2 - R_1^2)}\,dA$$

The normal force creates no moment about the $z$-axis, but the frictional force does: $dM = r\,dF$.

![ch08-Friction Slide 81](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-081.png)

Integrating over the entire bearing area:

$$M = \int r\,dF = \frac{\mu_s P}{\pi(R_2^2 - R_1^2)} \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_{R_1}^{R_2} r^2\,dr\,d\theta$$

$$\boxed{M = \frac{2}{3}\,\mu_s P\,\frac{R_2^3 - R_1^3}{R_2^2 - R_1^2}}$$

![ch08-Friction Slide 82](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-082.png)

**Special case:** For a solid pivot bearing ($R_2 = R$, $R_1 = 0$):

$$M = \frac{2}{3}\,\mu_s PR$$

> These equations apply only to bearings subjected to **constant pressure**. For non-uniform pressure, a pressure distribution must be determined.

## 8.7 Frictional Forces on Journal Bearings

![ch08-Friction Slide 85](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-085.png)

When a shaft or axle is subjected to lateral loads, a **journal bearing** is used for support. Provided the bearing is not or partially lubricated, the analysis can be based on the laws of dry friction.

![ch08-Friction Slide 86](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-086.png)

### Frictional Analysis

![ch08-Friction Slide 87](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-087.png)

If the vertical load is $P$, the bearing reactive force $R$ acting at the contact point is equal and opposite to $P$.

The moment needed to maintain constant rotation:

$$M = R\,r\sin\phi_k$$

![ch08-Friction Slide 88](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-088.png)

Define $r_f = r\sin\phi_k$. The dashed circle with radius $r_f$ is called the **friction circle**.

For a partially lubricated bearing, $\mu_k$ is small, so:

$$\sin\phi_k \approx \tan\phi_k \approx \mu_k$$

Therefore:

$$\boxed{M \approx R\,r\,\mu_k}$$

![ch08-Friction Slide 89](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-089.png)

![ch08-Friction Slide 90](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-090.png)

## 8.8 Rolling Resistance

![ch08-Friction Slide 91](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-091.png)

A rigid cylinder rolling at constant velocity along a rigid surface has the normal force perpendicular to the tangent at the point of contact. However, no materials are perfectly rigid. A hard cylinder will compress the soft surface underneath it.

![ch08-Friction Slide 92](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-092.png)

The magnitude of the force of deformation $N_d$ has a horizontal component that is always greater than that of restoration $N_r$. A horizontal driving force $P$ must be applied to maintain the motion.

![ch08-Friction Slide 93](assets/ch08-Friction/slide-093.png)

Considering the resultant normal pressure $N = N_d + N_r$ acting on the cylinder, to keep it in equilibrium rolling at constant rate, $N$ must be concurrent with $P$ and $W$.

Summing moments about $A$:

$$Wa = P(r\cos\theta)$$

Since $\cos\theta \approx 1$:

$$Wa \approx Pr$$

$$\boxed{a \approx \frac{Pr}{W} = r\,\mu_r}$$

where $a$ is the **coefficient of rolling resistance** $\mu_r$, having the dimension of length.

> Rolling resistance of railroad wheels on rails is small since steel is very stiff. By comparison, the rolling resistance of tractor wheels in a wet field is very large.
